"Magnus had a real bad case of low self-esteem. 

Even back when he was a kid, it drove his piano teachers crazy. The raw talent was obvious, the ear for music, the fast fingers and the competent left hand...but his confidence was practically non-existent. He refused to get on stage or play in a band and excel at something he was obviously meant to do. And wanted to. 

Years slipped by, he faded into the background and settled for jobs that didn't demand much of his actual capabilities. He had simply accepted the story that he wasn't meant for anything greater. Whenever that old spark flared up, whenever he felt the urge to reach out and actually build or do something ambitious, a visceral sinking feeling like a gray cloud just settled in and over him and blind him to the possiblities until he let go and surrendered the idea. 

Through what he perceived as a miracle he managed to land a gig as a composer for a game. One afternoon during a meeting, the team he was working with were analyzing their steam next fest demo period. Magnus expressed that the steam reviews didn't like the music that he had produced and the team checked him. 
"What do you mean", said the team, "I'm not reading that, where are you reading that?". His team even found evidence to the contrary.

Suddenly something clicked, he recognized and clearly saw the critical voice saying things like "You aren't any good. You can't do it. They don't like you. Just give up". 
He had always just belived this to be the truth and had never viewed this harmful self-talk as something separate. 

He remembered how he had wanted to express himself and really show what he could do in front of and together with others, but this voice spoke with such force that he never dared. 

Magnus had just met his inner critic."

In all my work with people, one of the things that surprises me over and over again is how much our internal critic slows us down and prevents us from having the life that we want. And I see this everywhere, whether it's the dozens of people that I get to work with in person, people who take these courses or in other professional collaborations, this internal critic has become something that is just stifling our lives. and weirdly at the exact same time they think they need it to be productive. So we're going to go into where that critic came from, what it is, what's actually going on there, how to work with it, and how that relationship with your internal critic can completely change if you do a few simple things. So before we get into it, let's first define inner critic.

So you can call it negative self talk, you can call it um critical voice in the head, you can just call it negative thoughts that you're having or thoughts that are telling you you have to do something to be better. It might say, "Hey, don't forget to do this. Don't forget to do this. You got to do this. Don't forget to do this while you're doing something." So it's literally nagging you to do something else while you're doing something and it's incessant or it's telling you, "Oh, you're no good. You should die. What's wrong with you? Why did you screw that up?" or it's constantly going back over the past saying, "Why didn't you do it this way? Why didn't you do it that way? Why didn't you do it this way? Or you could do this better? Why are you 20 years old and you don't have a girlfriend? Why is it that you're 40 years old and you don't have a house? Look at all these other people who can make money of their game and you can't." And so it's just whack, whack, whack over and over again with this negative self-t talk. So that's the thing that we're talking about. We're talking about having that voice in your head that's constantly riding you.

Now having an inner voice is normal, it's is called introspection and is different from this critical self-talk. Humans spend about one-third to one-half of waking life not in the present. It's so common that in sciency neurological terms it's called the default state. We do mental timetravel which is helpful for making sense of stuff and planning. The inner critic is more cyclical negative thoughts and emotions. The negative talk turns our normal capacity into a curse that affects performance, decision-making and is a common cause for stress and burnout. 

So if it sucks so much, why do we have it? 
To answer that question let's look back in time. Imagine two groups of your ancestors out roaming on the savanna with loin clothes and spears, you get the point. One of the groups are chill, laidback thinking that "eh don't worry, we don't have to go out hunting NOW we have plenty of food", "Damn look at Dave, he's sleeping instead of gathering berries what a legend". The other group is worried, unhappy, constantly looking out for threats and ways to improve, thinking they could do better all the time. "What if we can't find the zebras?", I'm so shit at hunting I have to get better at hunting.", "I'm tired but I need to fucking get out there and push to keep find more berries because what if we don't have enough food"

Which of the groups has a better chance at surviving? My money is on group 2. So from an evolutionary perspective, rumination and negative anticipation aren't bugs; they are features. Our ancestors survived because their brains constantly scanned past mistakes and catastrophic future scenarios to keep them alive on the savanna. The issue is that we don't live in that context and survival isn't an issue for us anymore, but our whole system is wired for it. That leads to us feeling threatened or stressed or even depressed without sometimes really understanding why.
